\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In three years, 3D vision--language models have changed almost everything about how they look at a scene and almost nothing about what they read. They are trained and tested on the same handful of referring corpora, and what such a model learns about space is bounded by what that text says about space. The benchmarks have been generous: on most of their sentences a model can do well without reading much geometry (\cref{sec:related}). This is not a failing of the models; the text did not ask it of them. We set out to measure how much of that text actually does.

We call a referring expression \emph{exact} when it fits its target set and nothing else: no distractor left in, no target left out. No published tool scores a 3D referring expression on this criterion, to our knowledge, so we built one: a blind reader and a deterministic verifier (\cref{sec:scorer}). Of the sentences it certifies, human annotators resolve \TODO{77}\% to exactly the target set. On ScanRefer and Multi3DRefer it certifies \TODO{19}\% of human-written sentences, and finds nothing to check in \TODO{53--56}\% (\cref{sec:data}). An ambiguous sentence is not a neutral training example: it tells the model that these words mean several places at once, and the model's picture of the scene blurs to match.

Seshat-Star rewrites these sentences so that they are unambiguous, rich and verifiable---and still read as something a person would say. A division of labour makes this possible (\cref{sec:pipeline}). Geometry computes the relations that are hard to see in a rendering and easy to get wrong---which row and which seat in it, where to stand so that \emph{left} means something---and hands them to a frontier model as evidence. The model, not a template, decides which to use and how to say them: diversity comes from the free text, checkability from the structured channel. These operators took most of the work, and everything else in the paper runs on them: the writer's evidence, the scorer's verifier and the benchmark.

Two design choices follow from what we learnt about the model along the way. The scorer checks every rewrite, but its verdicts are never fed back into the writer: the model does whatever its evidence tells it to, so the only safe evidence is evidence that is true. And the improvement is not the base model's cleverness: the same writer, given better geometry, locks its target more often.

We test what this does to models on 3D referring. The answer must be an object in the room, so a language prior cannot skip the scene as it can in question answering; and by counting same-class distractors, success on a sentence can be attributed to the class name or to the spatial cues.

Our contributions are: (i) a set of geometric operators, 42 verifiable predicates, that compute the spatial relations a rendering hides; (ii) built on them, a scorer that measures whether a 3D referring expression is exact, and Seshat-Star, a rewriting pipeline in which geometry supplies the evidence, a frontier model writes, and the scorer checks; (iii) the rewritten ScanRefer, Multi3DRefer, Nr3D and Sr3D, every sentence carrying the relations it uses, and a distractor-heavy benchmark; (iv) evidence that changing the training text alone makes a 3D grounding model sharper exactly where spatial cues are needed; (v) the ceiling: a frontier model's accuracy on the hardest targets under four levels of spatial information.
