\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In three years, 3D vision--language models have changed almost everything about how they look at a scene and almost nothing about what they read. They are trained and tested on the same handful of referring corpora, and what such a model learns about space is bounded by what that text says about space. The benchmarks have been generous: on most of their sentences a model can do well without reading much geometry (\cref{sec:related}). This is not a failing of the models; the text did not ask it of them. We set out to measure how much of that text actually does.

We call a referring expression \emph{exact} when it fits its target set and nothing else: no distractor left in, no target left out. No published tool scores a 3D referring expression on this criterion, to our knowledge, so we built one (\cref{sec:scorer}). Its reader is blind: it receives the sentence and an unmarked list of the room's objects, and is never told the target. Its verifier contains no model: 42 geometric predicates, evaluated on the point cloud. \TODO{On $n$ sentences its verdicts agree with human judgement $x$\% of the time.} On ScanRefer and Multi3DRefer it finds \TODO{19}\% of human-written sentences exact under a strict reading, in which a clause that is false of the target counts against the sentence, and \TODO{21}\% under a lenient one, in which such a clause is skipped; \TODO{53--56}\% offer nothing it can check. The two corpora also attach different labels to the same words (\cref{sec:related}). An ambiguous sentence is not a neutral training example: it tells the model that these words mean several places at once, and the model's picture of the scene blurs to match.

Seshat-Star rewrites these sentences so that they are unambiguous, rich and verifiable---and still read as something a person would say. A division of labour makes this possible. Geometry computes the relations that are hard to see in a rendering and easy to get wrong---which end of the room, which row and which seat in it, what rests on what, where to stand so that \emph{left} means something---and hands them to a frontier model as evidence. These operators, six families in all, took most of the work: each was revised against human inspection of real rooms until what it reported matched what a person sees, and everything else in the paper runs on them---the writer's evidence, the scorer's verifier and the benchmark. The model, not a template, decides which of them to use and how to say them, and keeps the annotator's own attributes and landmarks. Diversity comes from the free text and checkability from the structured channel: the writer names relations in some 2,400 ways, the verifier needs to recognise 42, and the sentences read as descriptions rather than as filled templates.

Two design choices follow from what we learnt about the model along the way. The scorer checks every rewrite, but its verdicts are never fed back into the writer: the model does whatever its evidence tells it to, so the only safe evidence is evidence that is true. And the improvement is not the base model's cleverness: an earlier version of the pipeline, with a menu of relations and a smaller model to phrase them, produced little worth keeping, and the same frontier writer given better geometry locks its target more often \TODO{(19 $\rightarrow$ 26 of 40)}.

We test what this does to models on 3D referring, for three reasons. The target is explicit, so success on a sentence can be attributed---by counting same-class distractors---to the class name or to the spatial cues. Shortcuts are harder to hide than in question answering, where the answer is a word and a language prior can skip the scene; here the answer must be an object in the room. And picking the right object among look-alikes is precisely the case in which the model has to digest the 3D information in the sentence.

Our contributions are: (i) a set of geometric operators---six families, 42 verifiable predicates---that compute the spatial relations a rendering hides; (ii) built on them, a scorer that measures whether any 3D referring expression is exact, and Seshat-Star, a rewriting pipeline in which geometry supplies the evidence, a frontier model writes, and the scorer checks, none doing another's job; (iii) a pipeline that runs on any real scene with instance masks, with or without an original sentence, and with it the rewritten ScanRefer, Multi3DRefer, Nr3D and Sr3D---every sentence carrying the relations it uses, both the predicates the verifier checked and the relations the writer reports---and a distractor-heavy benchmark; (iv) evidence that changing the training text alone makes a 3D grounding model sharper exactly where spatial cues are needed; (v) the ceiling: a frontier model's accuracy on the hardest targets under four levels of spatial information.
