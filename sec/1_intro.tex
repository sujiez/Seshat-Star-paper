\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In three years, 3D vision--language models have changed almost everything about how they look at a scene---point-cloud encoders, object tokens, video frames, geometry-aware position encodings---and almost nothing about what they read. They are trained and tested on the same handful of referring corpora, written by people describing one object in a room, and what such a model learns about space is bounded by what that text says about space. The benchmarks have been generous: a model can score well on them without reading much geometry at all (\cref{sec:related}). This is not a failing of the models; the text did not ask it of them. We set out to measure how much of that text actually does.

A referring expression is correct only if it fits its target set exactly: a sentence in which every word is true can still be wrong when it fits one object too many, or one too few. The first is a distractor the sentence failed to rule out; the second is a member of the target set it failed to cover. To our knowledge, no published tool scores an arbitrary 3D referring expression on this criterion, so we built one. A blind reader---a frontier model given the sentence and an unmarked list of the room's objects---translates it into predicates over object ids, and a deterministic verifier, 42 geometric predicates computed from the point cloud, evaluates them; the sentence is \emph{exact} when the objects satisfying every predicate are precisely the target set. \TODO{On $n$ sentences its verdicts agree with human judgement $x$\% of the time.} Applied to ScanRefer and Multi3DRefer, it finds that about one human-written sentence in five is exact, that over half contain no spatial relation that can be checked at all, and that 81\% of Multi3DRefer's multi-target sentences are ScanRefer sentences---written about one chair, labelled with four. An ambiguous sentence is not a neutral training example: it tells the model that these words mean several places at once, and the model's picture of the scene blurs to match.

Seshat-Star rewrites these sentences so that they are unambiguous, rich and verifiable---and still read as something a person would say. A division of labour makes this possible. Geometry computes the relations that are hard to see in a rendering and easy to get wrong---which end of the room, which row and which seat in it, what rests on what, where to stand so that \emph{left} means something---and hands them to a frontier model as evidence. These operators, six families and 42 predicates, took most of the work: each was revised against human inspection of real rooms until what it reported matched what a person sees, and everything else in the paper runs on them---the writer's evidence, the scorer's verifier and the benchmark. The model, not a template, decides which of them to use and how to say them, and keeps the annotator's own attributes and landmarks. Diversity comes from the free text and checkability from the structured channel: the writer names relations in some 2,400 ways, the verifier needs to recognise 42, and the sentences read as descriptions rather than as filled templates. The same scorer then checks the result.

Two design choices follow from what we learnt about the model along the way. The scorer's verdicts are never fed back into the writer: the model does whatever its evidence tells it to, so the only safe evidence is evidence that is true. And the improvement is not the base model's cleverness: an earlier version of the pipeline, with a menu of relations and a smaller model to phrase them, produced little worth keeping, and the same frontier writer given better geometry locks its target more often \TODO{(19 $\rightarrow$ 26 of 40)}.

We test what this does to models on 3D referring, for three reasons. The target is explicit, so success on a sentence can be attributed---by counting same-class distractors---to the class name or to the spatial cues. Shortcuts are harder to hide than in question answering, where the answer is a word and a language prior can skip the scene; here the answer must be an object in the room. And picking the right object among look-alikes is precisely the case in which the model has to digest the 3D information in the sentence.

Our contributions are: (i) a set of geometric operators---six families, 42 verifiable predicates---that compute the spatial relations a rendering hides; (ii) built on them, a scorer that measures whether any 3D referring expression is exact, and Seshat-Star, a rewriting pipeline in which geometry supplies the evidence, a frontier model writes, and the scorer checks, none doing another's job; (iii) a pipeline that runs on any real scene with instance masks, with or without an original sentence, and with it the rewritten ScanRefer, Multi3DRefer, Nr3D and Sr3D---every sentence carrying the relations it uses, both the predicates the verifier checked and the relations the writer reports---and a distractor-heavy benchmark; (iv) evidence that changing the training text alone makes a 3D grounding model sharper exactly where spatial cues are needed; (v) the ceiling: a frontier model's accuracy on the hardest targets under four levels of spatial information.
