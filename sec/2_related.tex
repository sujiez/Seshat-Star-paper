\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Generated referring language for 3D scenes.}
Most machine-generated 3D referring text comes from one recipe: a scene graph or a program holds the geometry, a template or a constraint sampler turns it into a specification that rules out every distractor, and a language model paraphrases the result~\cite{referit3d,sceneverse,3dgrand,disc3d,clevr3dderef,clevrrefplus}; ViGiL3D++~\cite{vigil3d,vigil3dpp}, the closest work to ours, samples its constraints from real scans. These pipelines agree on three things. Uniqueness is guaranteed before the text exists, and after the paraphrase nothing is checked against the geometry again. The language model changes the words, not what is said, and what can be said is thinnest exactly where human sentences need help: viewpoints, rows and stacks. And nobody scores the finished sentence; where the authors checked by hand, fewer than half of the sentences whose target has look-alikes were valid~\cite{vigil3dpp}. We differ on all three counts. The model decides what to add to a sentence a person wrote; verification comes \emph{after} the text, on the final sentence; and every sentence receives a score, which is how we can report a number for ScanRefer itself---something no construction-time guarantee can do. Beacon3D~\cite{beacon3d} documents the same ambiguity by a human study and re-annotates thirty scenes to test on; we measure it geometrically and repair the training corpora at scale.

\paragraph{The corpora.}
ScanRefer~\cite{scanrefer} attaches one description to one object; Nr3D~\cite{referit3d} does the same through a reference game; Multi3DRefer~\cite{multi3drefer} extends ScanRefer to zero, one or several targets, and labels a description that matches several objects with all of them. A plural reference and an under-specified one thus receive the same kind of label, and the two corpora share words while disagreeing about what they mean: 81\% of Multi3DRefer's multi-target validation sentences paraphrase ScanRefer sentences about the same scene, labelled with one chair in one corpus and with four in the other. Neither corpus breaks its own rules; the sentence is under-specified and each convention resolves it differently. Nanri \etal~\cite{nanri2026viewpoint} observe that neither corpus handles observer-dependent relations, and supply the viewpoint to the model as a camera pose; we write the standpoint into the sentence instead, so that no input channel is added.

\paragraph{Models, and where geometry enters.}
Recent 3D grounding models admit geometry in different ways---object tokens without coordinates~\cite{chatscene}, rotary encodings of object centres~\cite{quatrope}, world coordinates added to visual features~\cite{video3dllm,3drs}, shape features with a distance bias~\cite{3dllava}---and their ScanRefer scores sit in one band whichever way they choose. Third parties have reached the same conclusion from the other side: a language model that never sees the scene scores unexpectedly well on SQA3D~\cite{sqa3d,beacon3d}, and 3D-LLaVA fails on expressions that contain nothing but a direction~\cite{nanri2026viewpoint}. Our reading is not that these models cannot use geometry but that the text they are scored on seldom requires it; \cref{sec:model} asks what happens when it does, with the architecture of 3D-LLaVA untouched\TODO{; further architectures pending}. Training-free methods have begun to repair the text at test time: TDVR~\cite{tdvr} has an LLM disambiguate each query and infer the viewpoint the sentence left out, inside one grounding method. We repair the corpus once, for any model trained on it, and measure that the repair holds. Frontier models are usually measured on spatial question answering~\cite{vqa2,vsibench}; GR3D~\cite{gr3d_zillow} hands GPT-5 a scene's geometry as text indexed by object identifiers, the identifier-bound design our writer and reader rely on. We measure them on grounding instead (\cref{sec:model}).
