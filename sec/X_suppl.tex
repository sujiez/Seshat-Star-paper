\paragraph{What this section adds.}
The paper trains three models twice each and reports their overall figures.
This section gives how they were trained and scored; every tier of the benchmark and every cell of the full validation sets, now including the rewrite-trained 3DRS, which the paper could test on the benchmark only; the swap test in full; the same results split by the number of distractors and by the number of targets, with paired tests item by item; the number of boxes the two models that choose among boxes return; and, for 3D-LLaVA, the rise at Acc@0.5 separated into finding the target and fitting it.

\paragraph{Corrections to the paper.}
% Where each figure is in the paper: tab/model.tex (3DRS, hard, rew.: 62.1, 60.5); sec/6 "5 and 7 (3DRS)"; abstract and sec/1 "5 to 24".
% Final weights: tab/supp_hard.tex (60.2, 61.6; 154 against 145 of 256 = 3.5 points; 61.6 - 53.3 = 8.3).
Two things in the paper change with these results.
First, the paper's figures for the rewrite-trained 3DRS come from the checkpoint at step 13,000 of 16,266, the latest that existed when the paper was due, and the paper does not say so.
With the final weights its two figures on the benchmark in \cref{tab:model}, 62.1 and 60.5, become 60.2 and 61.6; its lead there, given in \cref{sec:model} as 5 and 7 points, becomes 4 and 8; and the range over the three models, given in the abstract and the introduction as 5 to 24 points, becomes 4 to 24.
All three rewrite-trained models still lead on the benchmark, and no other figure of the paper depends on this checkpoint; the cells that \cref{tab:model} leaves empty for 3DRS are filled in \cref{tab:supp_val}.
Second, the paper says (\cref{sec:model}) that training on the rewrites ``changes not how often the right object is found from an original sentence, but what a model does when the sentence fits several''.
The first half is too strong: over all ScanRefer items the rewrite-trained models find the target 0.5 (3D-LLaVA), 1.2 (QuatRoPE) and 1.5 (3DRS) points less often than the original-trained ones, and the nearly level figure of 3D-LLaVA is a loss when the target has exactly one same-class distractor set against a gain in crowded rooms.
The sentence should read: training on the rewrites leaves the share of targets found from an original sentence level or slightly lower, and changes what a model does when the sentence fits several.
The second half stands, and the paragraphs below add to its evidence; the abstract's statement, which is about 3D-LLaVA on ScanRefer alone, is not affected.

\paragraph{Training.}
Each model is trained twice from the same public initialisation with its authors' released code and one seed.
Apart from fixes to memory use and checkpointing, we changed two things, identically in both arms: the number of GPUs, and the list of training corpora, so that the three models train on the same ones---ScanRefer, Multi3DRefer, Nr3D and Sr3D for grounding, Scan2Cap and Nr3D for captioning.
The two arms read the same files row for row, and only the sentence differs; in the rewrite arm the caption targets are rewrites too, and four Multi3DRefer rows that have no rewrite keep the original.
Multi3DRefer's zero-target items are removed from the training set of both arms, and no score we report includes them.
3D-LLaVA is tuned with LoRA for one epoch (4,826 steps).
QuatRoPE is trained in its two stages, one epoch on ground-truth objects and three on Mask3D proposals, with LoRA of rank 16.
3DRS is fine-tuned in full for one epoch (16,266 steps).
We evaluate the last checkpoint of every run, with the one exception described below, and chose none by a validation score.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
3D-LLaVA outputs a mask and is scored by mask IoU; on Multi3DRefer its repository scores the mask against the union of the targets, which is not the official F1.
QuatRoPE and 3DRS choose among Mask3D proposals and are scored by box IoU, with the official F1 on Multi3DRefer.
The three blocks of \cref{tab:supp_hard,tab:supp_val} are therefore not comparable with one another; within a block, the rows are.
On items with several targets the two kinds of score also reward different behaviour.
Against the union of the targets, a mask spread over every object of the class scores well whenever the targets are most of them, and a mask on one target of $n$ scores about $1/n$.
F1 counts each returned box as right or wrong, so one right box of $n$ earns $2/(n+1)$ and boxes on distractors cost precision.
A mask can therefore hedge where a list of boxes cannot, and 3D-LLaVA's figures on multi-target items move further in both directions than those of the other two models.

\paragraph{The benchmark by tier.}
On the benchmark's sentences the rewrite-trained model leads in every single-target tier and in the two-target tier, for all three models (\cref{tab:supp_hard}).
The tier with three or more targets has 22 items: there 3D-LLaVA and QuatRoPE lead and 3DRS trails.
On the original sentences of the same items the lead shrinks or reverses: at Acc@0.5 it is 6 and 3 points for 3D-LLaVA, where it was 24 and 23, and QuatRoPE's rewrite arm is 1 and 3 points behind.
The lead on the benchmark thus needs the rewritten sentence as well as the model trained on rewrites.

\paragraph{3DRS: which checkpoint the paper reports.}
The checkpoint at step 13,000 and the final weights are both in \cref{tab:supp_hard}, tier by tier.
With the final weights the rewrite arm leads the original arm by 3.5 points on the ScanRefer items of the benchmark (154 against 145 of 256) and by 8.3 on the Multi3DRefer items.
Between the two checkpoints the ScanRefer figure moves by five items and the Multi3DRefer figure by 1.1 points the other way; with one run per arm we cannot tell such movement from noise.
The direction is the same at both checkpoints in every tier, including the one where the rewrite arm trails.
The original arm was evaluated at its final weights throughout.
The paper tests the rewrite-trained 3DRS on the benchmark only.
On the full validation sets the final weights give 76.1 and 68.1 on the ScanRefer rewrites, against 71.5 and 63.8 for the original arm, and 61.8 and 55.2 on the original sentences, against 63.3 and 56.5 (\cref{tab:supp_val}); on Multi3DRefer, 67.4 and 60.5 against 63.3 and 56.8 on the rewrites, and 57.7 and 51.8 against 59.8 and 53.7 on the originals.
The rewrite arm thus leads by 4 to 5 points on the rewrites and trails by 1 to 2 on the originals.

\paragraph{Swapped sentences.}
\Cref{tab:supp_val} also gives every cell of the swap test.
A swapped sentence still names the class, so targets without a distractor are still found (78 to 86\% at Acc@0.25); with distractors, every arm of the two models falls to between 34 and 41\%.
The one 3DRS cell we have, the original arm on swapped originals, falls likewise (78.0\% and 33.6\%).
Swapped rewrites keep the style and lose the content, and on them the rewrite arm is no better than the original arm (35.1 against 39.5 for 3D-LLaVA, 35.3 against 35.5 for QuatRoPE).
Its lead on the rewrites therefore comes from what a sentence says about its own scene, not from a familiar style.

\paragraph{On the original sentences, by number of distractors.}
% Source: Seshat-Star/analysis/_transfer_strata.py (prints every figure quoted here; --tex writes tab/supp_strata.tex).
% 3DRS: final weights of both arms on the full sets (3drs_rw_final/val_{orig,rw}, in since 11-24 05:17 Beijing).  At step 13,000 on
% the 2k subset the loss at one distractor was larger (-5.8, p = 0.005) and the one in crowded rooms less clear (-3.7, p = 0.10).
The paper reports that on the original ScanRefer sentences the rewrite-trained 3D-LLaVA finds the target as often as the original-trained one (Acc@0.25 61.0 against 61.5) and, reading it together with QuatRoPE, concludes that training on the rewrites does not change how often the right object is found from an original sentence.
The upper panel of \cref{tab:supp_strata} splits the same items by the number of same-class distractors, and the conclusion needs two qualifications.

First, when the target has exactly one distractor, all three models find it less often after training on the rewrites: by 3.5 points for 3D-LLaVA, 2.4 for QuatRoPE and 1.7 for 3DRS.
Among the items on which the two arms of a model give different verdicts, the rewrite arm loses 272 and gains 197 (3D-LLaVA), loses 243 and gains 192 (QuatRoPE), and loses 189 and gains 153 (3DRS); two-sided sign tests give $p < 0.001$, $p = 0.016$ and $p = 0.058$, so for 3DRS this loss is not established on its own.

Second, in crowded rooms the three models part.
With five or more distractors 3D-LLaVA finds the target more often after training on the rewrites (29.1 against 26.3; it gains 307 items and loses 244, $p = 0.008$).
QuatRoPE is level (26.2 against 26.8, $p = 0.57$), and 3DRS is lower (34.1 against 37.4; it gains 242 items and loses 317, $p = 0.002$).
The level average of 3D-LLaVA is therefore a loss at one distractor set against a gain in crowded rooms.
The two models that choose among boxes show no such gain, and their averages are lower by 1.2 and 1.5 points.
For 3D-LLaVA, Acc@0.5 on the original sentences still rises at every number of distractors, by 2.6 to 8.8 points; that is the tightening of masks described in the paper, and a model that chooses among boxes has no counterpart to it.

Asked with the rewrites, all three rewrite-trained models find more targets at every number of distractors, and their lead grows with crowding: it is 3.2, 4.7 and 2.1 points at one distractor and 18.2, 6.9 and 9.4 at five or more.
Each arm, asked with the other arm's sentences, is outside the style it was trained on, and the two crossings are not alike: over all ScanRefer items the original-trained models gain 6.0, 4.7 and 8.1 points when given the rewrites, and gain in every tier, while the rewrite-trained models, given the originals, fall 0.5, 1.2 and 1.5 points short of the original-trained ones.

Two cautions.
We drew these tiers for 3D-LLaVA after its overall figures were known, and expected the other two models to repeat its pattern; they do not repeat the gain in crowded rooms, and only QuatRoPE clearly repeats the loss at one distractor.
And each model was trained once per arm.
Multi3DRefer's single-target items give the same picture for each model, but they share scenes, objects and many sentences with ScanRefer and are not an independent replication.
We have no tested explanation for the loss at one distractor.
One might expect it to depend on how much an original sentence says about position; splitting the items by the number of spatial words in the original sentence, counted crudely against a list of words, shows such a trend for 3D-LLaVA (from $-3.1$ points with none to $+2.1$ with three or more) and none for the other two models, and we do not rely on it.

\paragraph{Several targets.}
The paper reports what the rewrites cost on Multi3DRefer's original multi-target sentences.
The lower panel of \cref{tab:supp_strata} splits those items by the number of targets.
On the original sentences the cost grows with the number of targets in all three models: for 3D-LLaVA from 4 points at two targets to 42 at five or more, for QuatRoPE from 7 to 17, for 3DRS from 1 to 11.
An original sentence seldom says how many objects are meant, and a model trained on the rewrites then answers with one: for these sentences the rewrite-trained QuatRoPE returns a single box in 96\% of cases, the original-trained one in 47\%.
Given the rewrite, which states the number, the same model returns 2.0 boxes on average at two targets, 3.4 at three or four (the labels average 3.4) and 8.0 at five or more (8.4); \cref{tab:supp_count} gives every cell.
The original-trained models, for their part, err towards the middle in both directions.
For an original sentence with one target and at least one distractor, QuatRoPE returns more than one box in 13\% of cases and 3DRS in 32\%; for one with five or more targets they return 3.3 and 2.9 boxes on average, where the labels have 8.4.

The converse holds as well.
The rewrite of such an item does say how many are meant, yet the original-trained models gain nothing from it overall (72.3 to 67.0 for 3D-LLaVA, 47.6 to 42.3 for QuatRoPE, 43.5 to 42.7 for 3DRS), and they lose where the targets are many: at five or more, 3D-LLaVA falls from 78.4 to 45.9, QuatRoPE from 36.4 to 26.6 and 3DRS from 31.8 to 25.7.
The counts show how: given a rewrite, the original-trained QuatRoPE answers with a single box for 76\% of these items, against 47\% for the original sentence.
On multi-target items neither the sentence nor the training is enough alone.
With both, 3D-LLaVA and QuatRoPE gain at every number of targets over the same model trained and asked with the originals, by 9 to 12 and by 13 to 21 points.
3DRS does not: it gains 6 points at two targets, loses 1 at three or four (35.3 against 36.6) and loses 9 at five or more (22.4 against 31.8).
Unlike QuatRoPE, its rewrite arm does not return as many objects as the rewrite states: it returns a single box for 74\% of these rewrites, as for 70\% of the originals, and 1.4 boxes on average where the labels have 3.3.
The same holds on the benchmark (1.6 boxes on the items with three or more targets, whose labels average 4.8), which is why that is the one tier in which the rewrite arm trails.
Nor does the number follow the sentence in the original arm, which returns 2.1 boxes on average for the multi-target originals and 2.0 for their rewrites.

The reason lies in how 3DRS returns boxes, not in the data: the training files of the two arms list the same target boxes in every row.
In the released code the model does not state a number.
The proposals are ranked by score and returned in that order until their probabilities add up to a fixed value (0.4 by default), so one confident choice ends the list; and the training loss asks only that the targets together receive the probability, which a single target can satisfy alone.
The number of boxes therefore measures how peaked the scores are, and the rewrite arm's are more peaked.
This costs it on items with several targets and spares it surplus boxes on items with one: for an original sentence with one target and a distractor it returns more than one box in 21\% of cases, where the original arm does in 32\%.
We ran the released code unchanged in both arms and report what it returns; why the rewrite arm's scores are more peaked we have not tested.

\paragraph{Finding the target and fitting it.}
Call a target found when the mask reaches an IoU of 0.25 and fitted when it reaches 0.5; Acc@0.5 is then the share found times the share of those that are fitted (\cref{tab:supp_diag}).
Training on the rewrites changes fitting and not finding: asked with the originals, the share fitted goes from 64.4\% to 78.1\% while Acc@0.25 stays level.
The sentence changes finding and hardly fitting: the original arm, asked with the rewrites, finds 61.6\% in place of 54.6\% and fits 66.8\% of them.
Along the diagonal, from 35.2 to 59.8 at Acc@0.5, the two contribute about equally: in logarithms, 52\% of the rise is finding and 48\% fitting.
Item by item, of the 3,518 targets the original arm did not find from the original sentence, the rewrite arm fits 38\% from the rewrite; of the 2,725 it fitted, 89\% stay fitted and 7\% are no longer found.
Multi3DRefer's single-target items with distractors behave alike (38.5 to 64.3, 49\% of it finding).
The rewrite arm also misses outright more often on the originals (39.1\% against 33.1\%): a mask that commits to one object is wholly wrong when the object is the wrong one.

\paragraph{The sample behind the three rates of the scorer section.}
The paper reports how often GPT-6 resolves sentences the scorer certifies strictly, only leniently, or not at all.
The sample is the single-target candidates from which the benchmark was drawn, each a rewrite with the target's attribute words removed.
The strictly certified ones are the benchmark's 546 single-target items, of which 536 returned an answer; 248 further candidates are certified only leniently and 349 not at all.
Given every object's label and position, GPT-6 resolves 98.1\%, 95.6\% and 89.7\% of the three groups; given unlabelled renderings instead, 76.3\%, 75.4\% and 69.1\%.
The groups differ in difficulty---chance is 21.2\%, 18.2\% and 13.4\%---so the fall from 98 to 90 mixes the scorer's verdict with the number of distractors.
Within one tier the order holds more weakly: 98.8\%, 96.0\% and 96.0\% at two to four distractors, 96.8\%, 95.1\% and 87.1\% at five or more.
We claim the order, not the size of the gap.

\paragraph{Beyond the vocabulary.}
Of the relations the writer reports, 13.1\% are unknown to the verifier: 6.2\% are attributes and comparisons, which we leave unscored on purpose, and 6.9\% are spatial relations it has no predicate for, chiefly the faces and ends of furniture, \emph{facing} and \emph{across from}.
Sentences whose relations verify and sentences whose relations do not carry them equally often (56\% and 58\%).

\paragraph{Objects outside the label set.}
The rewrites mention an object outside the scene's label set in 15.2\% of Multi3DRefer and 17.0\% of ScanRefer validation sentences, the originals in 16.3\% and 18.2\%.
At most 4.6\% and 4.7\% of the rewrites introduce an object word found neither in the original nor in the labels, usually a finer name for a labelled object.

\paragraph{Writing without an original.}
Without an original sentence the writer's prompt loses the paragraphs that mention one, and colour and material words are disallowed, since no annotator vouches for them.
\Cref{tab:gen} gives the certified rates by scene source; the Matterport3D sample is a single house and stands only as an existence proof.

\paragraph{Annotators.}
The four annotators are students known to the authors; none is an author.
Each was asked individually and agreed to take part.
They were paid per item, at one of three fixed rates according to the task: selecting the objects a sentence fits, deciding whether a sentence has a referent, and judging a single statement.
The tasks ask for judgements about sentences and scenes; no personal data about the annotators was collected and none is reported.
For that reason no review by an ethics board was sought.
\paragraph{Predicate by predicate.}
We also checked the verifier on the structures we define ourselves: rows, furniture faces and stacks, with plain adjacency as a control.
Annotators judged single statements with the objects marked.

\subsection{Sentences with several targets}
\label{supp:parts}
% Source: spatial/compose.py verify_reader_parts (blind reader) / verify_parts (writer).
A sentence about several objects often coordinates groups, each pinned by its own relations (\emph{the two at the head of the near bed and the one on the far bed}).
The reader therefore returns \emph{common} predicates, which hold for the whole referent, and a list of \emph{parts}, each with its own predicates and, when the sentence states one, a count.
Let $B$ be the candidates that satisfy every common predicate ($B = C$ if there is none), and $S_j$ the members of $B$ that satisfy every predicate of part $j$.
The referent is the union of the parts,
\begin{equation}
  R = \bigcup_j S_j ,
  \label{eq:parts}
\end{equation}
or $R = B$ when the sentence has no parts, and the sentence is certified when at least one predicate was evaluated and $R = T$.
Union, not intersection: the pillow on the left bed need not be on the right bed, so an object joins the referent by satisfying one part.
Parts may overlap, and a part may cover fewer members than it seems to if another part makes up the difference.
What no part may do is admit a distractor, since anything that survives one part survives in $R$.
The strict score also requires every stated count to equal the size of the set it describes; the lenient score skips predicates that hold for no target and ignores counts.
A single-target sentence is the special case of no parts and $|T| = 1$, which gives \cref{eq:exact}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition 1 and sensitivity}
\label{supp:proof}
% Source: Seshat-Star/docs/VALIDATION_AND_EXPERIMENTS.md §1.2; RESULTS §4.1--4.2 (numbers after the 11-12 correction).
Recall that $\hat{E}_k$ is the set of candidates the verifier returns for predicate $p_k$ and $E_k$ the set that truly satisfies it; $T$ is the target set and $D$ the distractors.
Assume (A1) $\hat{E}_k \cap T \subseteq E_k$ and (A2) $E_k \cap D \subseteq \hat{E}_k$ for every $k$, and $\bigcap_k \hat{E}_k = T$.

\emph{$T \subseteq \bigcap_k E_k$.}
Take $t \in T$.
Since $T = \bigcap_k \hat{E}_k$, $t \in \hat{E}_k$ for every $k$, hence $t \in \hat{E}_k \cap T \subseteq E_k$ by (A1).

\emph{$\bigcap_k E_k \subseteq T$.}
Take $x \in \bigcap_k E_k$ and suppose $x \notin T$, so $x \in D$.
Then $x \in E_k \cap D \subseteq \hat{E}_k$ for every $k$ by (A2), hence $x \in \bigcap_k \hat{E}_k = T$, a contradiction. \hfill$\square$

The converse fails: if a distractor truly satisfies every predicate and the verifier wrongly rejects it on one, the sentence is certified and is not exact.
That is a violation of (A2), and it is the error to guard against.
(A1) and (A2) restrict errors in opposite directions on complementary sets, so a bound on the verifier's overall error rate implies neither.
The proposition takes the predicates as given and does not cover the reader.

\paragraph{How fragile is a certificate?}
For a certified sentence and a distractor $d$, let $m(d)$ be the number of predicates that exclude $d$.
If the verifier wrongly excludes a distractor with probability at most $q$ per predicate, independently across predicates, the probability that the certificate is false is at most
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{d \in D} q^{\,m(d)} ,
  \label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
by a union bound over distractors.
On the Multi3DRefer validation rewrites, 13.5\% of the distractors of certified sentences are excluded by a single predicate and 58.2\% by three or more; 17.0\% of certified sentences have a distractor that hangs on one predicate.
\Cref{eq:margin}, averaged over these sentences, gives at most 3.5\%, 7.2\% and 14.5\% false certificates for $q = 0.05$, $0.10$ and $0.20$, most of it from the sentences with a single-predicate distractor.
