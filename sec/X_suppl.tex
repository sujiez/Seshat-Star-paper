\paragraph{Terms.}
A \emph{distractor} is an object of the target's class in the same scene, other than the target; classes are matched by the head noun of the label.
Each model is trained twice on the same items: once with the original sentences of the training corpora, which we call the \emph{original arm} or the original-trained model, and once with our rewrites of those sentences, the \emph{rewrite arm} or the rewrite-trained model.
Either arm can be asked with the original validation sentences or with their rewrites; the items and their labels are the same in both cases, so each model has four cells.
Acc@0.25 and Acc@0.5 are the shares of items whose predicted mask or box has an IoU of at least 0.25 or 0.5 with the target, and we say that a target is \emph{found} at 0.25.
A \emph{tier} is the group of items with a given number of distractors or of targets, and a room is \emph{crowded} for a target with five or more distractors.
Where three figures are given in a row without names, they are for 3D-LLaVA, QuatRoPE and 3DRS, in that order.

\paragraph{What this section adds.}
The paper trains three models twice each and reports their overall figures.
This section says how they were trained and scored, and gives every tier of the benchmark and every cell of the full validation sets; these now include the rewrite-trained 3DRS, which the paper could test on the benchmark only.
It then gives the swap test in full; the same results split by the number of distractors and by the number of targets, with paired tests item by item; the number of boxes returned by the two models that choose among boxes; and, for 3D-LLaVA, how much of the rise at Acc@0.5 is finding the target and how much is fitting it.

\paragraph{What the three models share.}
% Every figure here is repeated, with its table, in the paragraphs below (analysis/_transfer_strata.py; tab/supp_hard, supp_val, supp_strata).
The three models take in the scene as a point cloud, as object tokens and as posed video; one answers with a mask and two choose among boxes; two are tuned with LoRA and one in full.
With 3DRS now run on the full sets, the comparison of the two arms comes out alike in all three, in five respects.
Given the rewrites in place of the originals, the original-trained models find more targets with no retraining, at every number of distractors (by 6.0, 4.7 and 8.1 points of Acc@0.25 over all ScanRefer validation items).
The rewrite-trained models, asked with the same rewrites, find more again (by a further 8.8, 4.4 and 4.6 points), and their lead over the original-trained models is larger in crowded rooms than with a single distractor.
On the benchmark the rewrite-trained model leads in every single-target tier and in the two-target tier.
Asked with the original sentences, the rewrite-trained model finds the target as often as the original-trained one or slightly less often (by 0.5, 1.2 and 1.5 points).
And on Multi3DRefer's original multi-target sentences, the rewrite-trained model loses more the more targets a sentence has.
The models part in two places, both set out below: only 3D-LLaVA gains on the original sentences in crowded rooms; and only 3DRS does not gain on multi-target items when it is both trained on the rewrites and asked with them, because of the rule by which its released code decides how many boxes to return.
Each arm of each model was trained once. The replication we have is across models: three pairs of runs that agree.

\paragraph{Corrections to the paper.}
% Where each figure is in the paper: tab/model.tex (3DRS, hard, rew.: 62.1, 60.5); sec/6 "5 and 7 (3DRS)"; abstract and sec/1 "5 to 24".
% Final weights: tab/supp_hard.tex (60.2, 61.6; 154 against 145 of 256 = 3.5 points; 61.6 - 53.3 = 8.3).
Two things in the paper change with these results.
First, the paper's figures for the rewrite-trained 3DRS come from the checkpoint at step 13,000 of 16,266, the latest that existed when the paper was due, and the paper does not say so.
With the final weights its two figures on the benchmark in \cref{tab:model}, 62.1 and 60.5, become 60.2 and 61.6; its lead there, given in \cref{sec:model} as 5 and 7 points, becomes 4 and 8; and the range over the three models, given in the abstract and the introduction as 5 to 24 points, becomes 4 to 24.
All three rewrite-trained models still lead on the benchmark, and no other figure of the paper depends on this checkpoint; the cells that \cref{tab:model} leaves empty for 3DRS are filled in \cref{tab:supp_val}.
Second, the paper says (\cref{sec:model}) that training on the rewrites ``changes not how often the right object is found from an original sentence, but what a model does when the sentence fits several''.
The first half is too strong: asked with the original ScanRefer validation sentences, the rewrite-trained models find the target 0.5 (3D-LLaVA), 1.2 (QuatRoPE) and 1.5 (3DRS) points less often than the original-trained ones, at Acc@0.25.
For 3D-LLaVA the small difference is the sum of two opposite ones: it finds 3.5 points fewer of the targets that have exactly one same-class distractor and 2.8 points more of those that have five or more.
The sentence should read: training on the rewrites leaves the share of targets found from an original sentence level or slightly lower, and changes what a model does when the sentence fits several.
The second half stands, and the paragraphs below add to its evidence; the abstract's statement, which is about 3D-LLaVA on ScanRefer alone, is not affected.

\paragraph{Training.}
Each model is trained twice from the same public initialisation with its authors' released code and one seed.
We changed the code only where a run would otherwise not start or not finish on our machines, identically in both arms.
3DRS's loader keeps the features of every scene it has read in memory, which over an epoch exceeds our memory limit, and we read them from disk each time; 3D-LLaVA runs with fewer data-loading processes for the same reason; and QuatRoPE and 3DRS save their training state more often, so that an interrupted run can continue.
% LOG.md (paper_materials_1113) 11-10 01:20, 11-11 19:40, 11-12 04:10; CHECK_3drs_m3d_train_targets_1124.md \S4.2: llava/video_utils.py is the only tracked 3DRS source file changed.
None of these touches the model, the loss or the data.
Two settings differ from the released recipes, again identically in both arms: the number of GPUs, and the list of training corpora, so that the three models train on the same ones---ScanRefer, Multi3DRefer, Nr3D and Sr3D for grounding, Scan2Cap and Nr3D for captioning.
The two arms read the same files, row for row, and differ only in the sentence.
In the rewrite arm every row but four has our rewrite in place of the original sentence, as the question in the grounding corpora and as the answer in the captioning corpora.
% REWRITE_ARM_REPORT.md: six training files, 240,444 rows, 240,440 replaced; kept_ids.json m3drefer_{3480,8926,28384,29953}, scene0305_00 objects [1,2], not zero-target.
Multi3DRefer's zero-target items are removed from the training set of both arms, and no score we report includes them.
3D-LLaVA is tuned with LoRA for one epoch (4,826 steps).
QuatRoPE is trained in its two stages, one epoch on ground-truth objects and three on Mask3D proposals, with LoRA of rank 16.
3DRS is fine-tuned in full for one epoch (16,266 steps).
Every figure in this supplement is from the last checkpoint of its run, unless a table says otherwise; no checkpoint was chosen by a validation score.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
3D-LLaVA outputs a mask and is scored by mask IoU; on Multi3DRefer its repository scores the mask against the union of the targets, which is not the official F1.
QuatRoPE and 3DRS choose among Mask3D proposals and are scored by box IoU, with the official F1 on Multi3DRefer.
A model that chooses among proposals cannot score above what the proposals allow: 3DRS has 50 for each scene, and for 90.5\% of the ScanRefer validation sentences one of them reaches an IoU of 0.25 with the target, for 82.1\% an IoU of 0.5.
% nozt_v2_per_sample_1124/beacon3d/DATA_NOTES_from_cluster.md \S4 (computed from the proposal table, without any model output); the same table gives 83.7 / 73.0 on Beacon3D.
The three blocks of \cref{tab:supp_hard,tab:supp_val} are therefore not comparable with one another; within a block, the rows are.
On items with several targets the two kinds of score also reward different behaviour.
Scored against the union of the targets, a mask that covers every object of the class does well when most of those objects are targets, while a mask on a single target out of $n$ scores about $1/n$.
The official F1 matches the returned boxes to the targets at the IoU threshold and, for each item, takes the harmonic mean of precision and recall.
One right box where there are $n$ targets has a precision of 1 and a recall of $1/n$, which gives $2/(n+1)$, and every box on a distractor lowers the precision.
A mask can therefore hedge, by spreading over several objects of the class, where a list of boxes cannot; this is why 3D-LLaVA's figures on multi-target items move further in both directions than those of the other two models.

\paragraph{The benchmark by tier.}
On the benchmark's sentences the rewrite-trained model leads in every single-target tier and in the two-target tier, for all three models (\cref{tab:supp_hard}).
The tier with three or more targets has 22 items: there 3D-LLaVA and QuatRoPE lead and 3DRS trails.
Asked with the original sentences of the same items, the rewrite-trained model's lead shrinks or reverses.
For 3D-LLaVA it is 6 points on the ScanRefer items and 3 on the Multi3DRefer items at Acc@0.5, against 24 and 23 with the benchmark's sentences; QuatRoPE's rewrite arm falls 1 and 3 points behind its original arm.
The lead on the benchmark thus needs the rewritten sentence as well as the model trained on rewrites.

\paragraph{3DRS: which checkpoint the paper reports.}
The checkpoint at step 13,000 and the final weights are both in \cref{tab:supp_hard}, tier by tier.
With the final weights the rewrite arm leads the original arm by 3.5 points on the ScanRefer items of the benchmark (154 against 145 of 256) and by 8.3 on the Multi3DRefer items.
From step 13,000 to the end of training the rewrite arm loses five of the ScanRefer items (159 to 154 of 256) and gains 1.1 points on the Multi3DRefer items; with one run per arm we cannot tell such movement from noise.
At both checkpoints the rewrite arm leads in the same tiers and trails in the same one.
The original arm was evaluated at its final weights throughout.
The paper tests the rewrite-trained 3DRS on the benchmark only.
On the full validation sets the final weights give Acc@0.25 and Acc@0.5 of 76.1 and 68.1 on the ScanRefer rewrites, against 71.5 and 63.8 for the original arm, and 61.8 and 55.2 on the original sentences, against 63.3 and 56.5 (\cref{tab:supp_val}).
On Multi3DRefer the F1 at the two thresholds is 67.4 and 60.5 against 63.3 and 56.8 on the rewrites, and 57.7 and 51.8 against 59.8 and 53.7 on the originals.
The rewrite arm thus leads by 4 to 5 points on the rewrites and trails by 1 to 2 on the originals.

\paragraph{Swapped sentences.}
The swap test asks whether a model reads what a sentence says about its scene.
Every validation sentence is replaced by one written for an object of the same class in another scene, while the items, their order and their labels stay as they are.
A swapped sentence thus names the right class and, except by chance, says nothing true about where the target is.
\Cref{tab:supp_val} gives every cell of the test, for swapped originals and for swapped rewrites.
Since a swapped sentence still names the class, most targets without a distractor are still found (76 to 86\% at Acc@0.25); with distractors, both arms of all three models fall to between 32 and 41\%.

\emph{Chance.}
A choice at random among the objects of the target's class would find 41.0\% of the targets with distractors.
Every swapped cell is at or below that figure: given a sentence about another scene, the models do no better than chance within the class, so whatever they find above chance comes from what the sentence says.
For 3DRS we have the results under the swap item by item, and \cref{tab:supp_swap} splits them by number of distractors.
Its four swapped cells stay within a few points of chance in every tier: they find 43 to 46\% of the targets with one distractor, where chance is 50\%, 24 to 26\% of those with two to four (chance 27.6\%) and 10 to 11\% of those with five or more (chance 9.4\%).

\emph{What the content of a sentence adds.}
The difference between a cell and its swapped counterpart is what the content of the sentence adds to its class word.
On ScanRefer targets with distractors, at Acc@0.25, the content of an original sentence adds 13.9, 13.3 and 24.4 points for the models trained on originals.
The content of a rewrite adds 36.8, 24.9 and 41.2 points for the models trained on rewrites.
It is worth more than that of an original even to a model that saw no rewrite in training: for the original-trained models it adds 22.1, 19.4 and 35.0 points.
For 3DRS the gap widens with crowding: with five or more distractors the original sentence adds 26 points for the original arm, and the rewrite adds 48 for the rewrite arm, over a chance of 9.4\%.
% (matched - swapped), ScanRefer `multiple', tab:supp_val: 54.6-40.7, 49.4-36.1, 58.0-33.6; 71.9-35.1, 60.2-35.3, 73.0-31.8; 61.6-39.5, 54.9-35.5, 67.5-32.5.  3DRS 5+: 37.4-11.1, 58.4-10.1.

\emph{Style.}
Swapped rewrites have the length and the style of rewrites but describe another scene.
If the rewrite arm did better on rewrites merely because they look like the sentences it was trained on, it would do better on swapped rewrites too.
It does not.
Against the original arm on the same swapped rewrites, Acc@0.25 on ScanRefer targets with distractors is 35.1 against 39.5 for 3D-LLaVA, 35.3 against 35.5 for QuatRoPE and 31.8 against 32.5 for 3DRS.
Against its own score on swapped originals, which are not in its training style, it is 35.1 against 37.0, 35.3 against 34.2 and 31.8 against 33.2.
At Acc@0.5 the rewrite-trained 3DRS falls from 65.5 on the rewrites to 28.1 on swapped rewrites; the paper reports 59.8 to 27.6 for 3D-LLaVA and 54.7 to 31.6 for QuatRoPE.
Without the content, then, the style is worth nothing, and the rewrite arm's lead on the rewrites cannot be a shift in style alone.
The test does not separate the two when the content is right: a model trained on rewrites may read what a rewrite says more readily than a model that was not, and the paper's remark that the benchmark's style favours the models trained on it stands.

\emph{Targets without a distractor.}
These lose 5 to 18 points under the swap although the class word alone would find them, and most in the rewrite arm asked with swapped rewrites (17.5, 11.1 and 13.5 points).
This is what one would expect if a sentence about another room misleads a model, and misleads most the model that relies most on the sentence; we have not examined these items.

\paragraph{On the original sentences, by number of distractors.}
% Source: Seshat-Star/analysis/_transfer_strata.py (prints every figure quoted here; --tex writes tab/supp_strata.tex).
% 3DRS: final weights of both arms on the full sets (3drs_rw_final/val_{orig,rw}, in since 11-24 05:17 Beijing).  At step 13,000 on
% the 2k subset the loss at one distractor was larger (-5.8, p = 0.005) and the one in crowded rooms less clear (-3.7, p = 0.10).
The paper reports that on the original ScanRefer sentences the rewrite-trained 3D-LLaVA finds the target as often as the original-trained one (Acc@0.25 61.0 against 61.5).
Reading this together with QuatRoPE's figures, it concludes that training on the rewrites does not change how often the right object is found from an original sentence.
The upper panel of \cref{tab:supp_strata} splits the same items by the number of same-class distractors, and the conclusion needs two qualifications.

First, when the target has exactly one distractor, all three models find it less often after training on the rewrites: by 3.5 points for 3D-LLaVA, 2.4 for QuatRoPE and 1.7 for 3DRS.
Counting only the items that one arm of a model finds and the other does not, the rewrite arm loses 272 and gains 197 (3D-LLaVA), loses 243 and gains 192 (QuatRoPE), and loses 189 and gains 153 (3DRS).
A two-sided sign test asks how likely so uneven a split would be if each of these items were as likely to go one way as the other; it gives $p < 0.001$, $p = 0.016$ and $p = 0.058$, so for 3DRS this loss is not established on its own.

Second, in crowded rooms the three models part.
With five or more distractors 3D-LLaVA finds the target more often after training on the rewrites (Acc@0.25 of 29.1 against 26.3; it gains 307 items and loses 244, $p = 0.008$).
QuatRoPE is level (26.2 against 26.8, $p = 0.57$), and 3DRS is lower (34.1 against 37.4; it gains 242 items and loses 317, $p = 0.002$).
3D-LLaVA's level average therefore hides a loss at one distractor and a gain in crowded rooms.
The two models that choose among boxes show no such gain, and their averages are lower by 1.2 and 1.5 points.
For 3D-LLaVA, Acc@0.5 on the original sentences still rises at every number of distractors, by 2.6 to 8.8 points; that is the tightening of masks described in the paper, and a model that chooses among fixed boxes cannot tighten them.

Asked with the rewrites, all three rewrite-trained models find more targets than the original-trained ones at every number of distractors, and their lead grows with crowding: it is 3.2, 4.7 and 2.1 points at one distractor and 18.2, 6.9 and 9.4 at five or more.
Each arm can also be asked with the kind of sentence it was not trained on, and the two cases differ.
Given the rewrites, the original-trained models gain 6.0, 4.7 and 8.1 points over all ScanRefer items, and gain in every tier.
Given the originals, the rewrite-trained models fall 0.5, 1.2 and 1.5 points short of the original-trained ones.

Two cautions.
We drew these tiers for 3D-LLaVA after its overall figures were known, and expected the other two models to repeat its pattern; they do not repeat the gain in crowded rooms, and only QuatRoPE clearly repeats the loss at one distractor.
And each model was trained once per arm.
Multi3DRefer's single-target items give the same picture for each model, but they share scenes, objects and many sentences with ScanRefer and are not an independent replication.
We have no tested explanation for the loss at one distractor.

\paragraph{Several targets.}
The paper reports that on Multi3DRefer's original multi-target sentences the rewrite-trained models score lower.
The lower panel of \cref{tab:supp_strata} splits those items by the number of targets.
The loss grows with the number of targets in all three models: for 3D-LLaVA from 4 points at two targets to 42 at five or more, for QuatRoPE from 7 to 17, for 3DRS from 1 to 11.
An original sentence seldom says how many objects are meant, and a model trained on the rewrites then answers with one: for these sentences the rewrite-trained QuatRoPE returns a single box in 96\% of cases, the original-trained one in 47\%.
Given the rewrite, which states the number, the same model returns 2.0 boxes on average at two targets, 3.4 at three or four (the labels average 3.4) and 8.0 at five or more (8.4); \cref{tab:supp_count} gives every cell.
The original-trained models err in both directions: too many boxes when there is one target, too few when there are many.
For an original sentence with one target and at least one distractor, QuatRoPE returns more than one box in 13\% of cases and 3DRS in 32\%; for one with five or more targets they return 3.3 and 2.9 boxes on average, where the labels have 8.4.

The converse holds as well.
The rewrite of such an item does say how many are meant, yet the original-trained models gain nothing from it: over all multi-target items their score at the 0.25 threshold goes from 72.3 to 67.0 (3D-LLaVA), from 47.6 to 42.3 (QuatRoPE) and from 43.5 to 42.7 (3DRS).
They lose most where the targets are many: at five or more, 3D-LLaVA falls from 78.4 to 45.9, QuatRoPE from 36.4 to 26.6 and 3DRS from 31.8 to 25.7.
The counts show how: given a rewrite, the original-trained QuatRoPE answers with a single box for 76\% of these items, against 47\% for the original sentence.
On multi-target items neither the sentence nor the training is enough alone.
Trained on the rewrites and asked with them, 3D-LLaVA and QuatRoPE score higher at every number of targets than the same model trained on and asked with the originals, by 9 to 12 and by 13 to 21 points.
3DRS does not: it gains 6 points at two targets, loses 1 at three or four (35.3 against 36.6) and loses 9 at five or more (22.4 against 31.8).
Unlike QuatRoPE, its rewrite arm does not return as many objects as the rewrite states: it returns a single box for 74\% of these rewrites, as for 70\% of the originals, and 1.4 boxes on average where the labels have 3.3.
The same holds on the benchmark (1.6 boxes on the items with three or more targets, whose labels average 4.8), which is why that is the one tier in which the rewrite arm trails.
Nor does the number follow the sentence in the original arm, which returns 2.1 boxes on average for the multi-target originals and 2.0 for their rewrites.

The reason lies in how 3DRS returns boxes, not in the data: the training files of the two arms list the same target boxes in every row.
In the released code the model does not state a number.
Each proposal receives a score, a softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and the proposals are returned in descending order of probability until the probabilities returned add up to a fixed value, 0.4 by default.
A proposal whose probability is 0.4 or more is therefore returned alone, whatever the sentence says about number.
The training loss is the negative logarithm of the total probability that falls on the targets.
It does not ask that each target receive a share, so it is as small when one target holds all the probability as when the targets divide it.
The number of boxes therefore measures how peaked the scores are, and the rewrite arm's are more peaked.
Peaked scores lose boxes where several are wanted and avoid surplus boxes where one is wanted: for an original sentence with one target and a distractor it returns more than one box in 21\% of cases, where the original arm does in 32\%.
We ran the released code unchanged in both arms and report what it returns; why the rewrite arm's scores are more peaked we have not tested.

\paragraph{Finding the target and fitting it.}
Call a target found when the mask reaches an IoU of 0.25 and fitted when it reaches 0.5; Acc@0.5 is then the share found times the share of those that are fitted (\cref{tab:supp_diag}).
Training on the rewrites changes fitting and not finding: asked with the originals, the share fitted goes from 64.4\% to 78.1\% while Acc@0.25 stays level (54.6 and 54.3).
The sentence changes finding and hardly fitting: the original arm, asked with the rewrites, finds 61.6\% in place of 54.6\% and fits 66.8\% of them.
From the original arm asked with the originals to the rewrite arm asked with the rewrites, Acc@0.5 rises from 35.2 to 59.8, and the two contribute about equally.
Because Acc@0.5 is a product, we divide its rise on a logarithmic scale: Acc@0.25 rises from 54.6 to 71.9 and the share fitted from 64.4\% to 83.1\%, and the logarithms of these two ratios are 52\% and 48\% of the logarithm of the ratio of Acc@0.5.
Item by item, of the 3,518 targets the original arm did not find from the original sentence, the rewrite arm fits 38\% from the rewrite; of the 2,725 that the original arm fitted, 89\% stay fitted and 7\% are no longer found.
Multi3DRefer's single-target items with distractors behave alike (Acc@0.5 from 38.5 to 64.3, 49\% of the rise being finding by the same division).
A target is \emph{missed} when the IoU is below 0.05, that is, when the mask is on another object or empty.
Asked with the originals, the rewrite arm misses more often than the original arm (39.1\% against 33.1\%): a mask that commits to one object is wholly wrong when the object is the wrong one.

\paragraph{The sample behind the three rates of the scorer section.}
The paper reports how often GPT-6 resolves sentences the scorer certifies strictly, only leniently, or not at all.
The sample is the single-target candidates from which the benchmark was drawn, each a rewrite with the target's attribute words removed.
The strictly certified ones are the benchmark's 546 single-target items, for 536 of which GPT-6 returned an answer; 248 further candidates are certified only leniently and 349 not at all.
Given every object's label and position, GPT-6 resolves 98.1\%, 95.6\% and 89.7\% of the three groups; given unlabelled renderings instead, 76.3\%, 75.4\% and 69.1\%.
The groups differ in difficulty: a choice at random among the target and its distractors would be right in 21.2\%, 18.2\% and 13.4\% of cases. The fall from 98 to 90 therefore mixes the scorer's verdict with the number of distractors.
Within one tier the order holds more weakly: 98.8\%, 96.0\% and 96.0\% at two to four distractors, 96.8\%, 95.1\% and 87.1\% at five or more.
We claim the order, not the size of the gap.

\section{Sentences written by others}
\label{supp:external}
% Protocol fixed on 11-20 (CAMERA_READY.md): primary subset = target has a same-class object (ScanNet annotation) AND Beacon3D's
% own spatial tag; tiers fixed before any model is run; the full set is also reported; results are reported whichever way they fall.
% Sources: /data/sujie/horus/_rerun/beacon3d/{REPORT.md (steps 1-7, 9), TIERS_FROZEN.md, ANALYSIS_PLAN_STEP8.md};
% nozt_v2_per_sample_1124/beacon3d/{DATA_NOTES_from_cluster.md, */per_sample.jsonl}; Seshat-Star/analysis/_beacon_models.py.
% Not said in the text (user, 11-23): Beacon3D's sentences mostly begin with a capital letter (1,222 of 1,611), as the rewrites do
% and ScanRefer's lower-cased originals do not; both arms receive them unchanged (EVAL_INFO.txt of the 3D-LLaVA runs).
The benchmark of the paper is written in the style of our rewrites and filtered by our scorer, and the paper leaves open whether the lead of the rewrite-trained models extends to sentences written by people that say where the target is (\cref{sec:discussion}).
Beacon3D~\cite{beacon3d} is a test set whose sentences were written by annotators, who also marked which of them use a spatial relation; part of it is on ScanNet scenes.
% Beacon3D paper, App. A (checked 11-23): ``the human annotator is supposed to fill the referential text ... and then select the involved knowledge types and a list of objects that match the referential text''.
This section scores them with our scorer and gives them to GPT-6, next to sentences of ours for the same objects, and then asks the trained models with them.
We set figures side by side and pass no judgement on Beacon3D.

\paragraph{The sentences and the subset.}
Beacon3D's ScanNet part has 1,611 sentences on 16 validation scenes, none of which is in our training files.
For each object its annotators wrote chains of sentences, from the most specific to the least, and tagged each sentence with what it uses: the class, appearance, a spatial relation.
For 68 sentences the target has no label in our vocabulary, so that the number of distractors is not defined; these are left out.
Of the other 1,543, 842 have a target without a distractor, 677 one with one to four and 24 one with five or more.
Before any model was called we fixed a subset: the single-target sentences whose target has a distractor and which carry the spatial tag, 528 sentences on 202 objects.
For each object one of its sentences was drawn at random, and these 202 sentences are used wherever two sentences or two arms are compared object by object.
For the same 202 objects the writer wrote one sentence each from the scene and the target alone, as in \cref{tab:gen}; it did not see Beacon3D's sentence.

\paragraph{Under the scorer.}
The upper block of \cref{tab:supp_b3d_sent} has the two sentences of each object.
The scorer certifies 44.6\% of Beacon3D's sentences and 71.8\% of ours strictly, and 45.5\% and 97.0\% leniently.
Of the objects on which the strict verdicts differ, ours is the certified sentence for 76 and Beacon3D's for 21 (exact McNemar test, $p < 0.001$).
The difference lies in how much a sentence says: Beacon3D's have 6 words and the reader binds 0.7 relations in them, none at all in 38\%; ours have 44 words and 4.5 relations.
Over all 1,543 sentences that can be scored the certified rate is 29.9\%, and 61.9\% have no relation that the reader can bind.
These rates count spatial relations only.
A sentence that singles out its target by colour or shape is not certified, so the rate says how often the spatial content of a sentence suffices, not how often the sentence is ambiguous to a person.

\paragraph{Before GPT-6.}
GPT-6 is given four unlabelled renderings of the scene and the sentence, and marks a point; the point is mapped to an object, and the answer is right when that object's box has an IoU of at least 0.5 with the target's.
It is right for 66.8\% of Beacon3D's sentences and 80.2\% of ours; where the two differ, ours is the one resolved for 42 objects and Beacon3D's for 15 ($p < 0.001$).
A choice at random among the target and its distractors would be right for 39.4\%.
Only seven of the 202 objects have five or more distractors, and we draw no conclusion for crowded rooms.

\paragraph{Three sentences for one object.}
ScanRefer describes 95 of the 202 objects.
For each we drew one of its ScanRefer sentences at random and put it through the same reader, verifier and GPT-6 (lower block of \cref{tab:supp_b3d_sent}).
The draw is not symmetric: Beacon3D's sentence is drawn from those tagged as spatial, ScanRefer's from all the sentences of the object.
Under the scorer the three kinds are apart in every pair: 15.8\%, 51.6\% and 82.1\% are certified strictly.
ScanRefer's sentence is certified where Beacon3D's is not for 7 objects, and the reverse for 41; against ours the counts are 2 and 65 for ScanRefer and 7 and 36 for Beacon3D ($p < 0.001$ in each case).
GPT-6 resolves our sentence most often, 82.1\% against 73.7\% for ScanRefer's and 66.3\% for Beacon3D's.
Where two sentences differ, ours is the one resolved for 20 objects of 25 against Beacon3D's ($p = 0.004$) and for 14 of 20 against ScanRefer's ($p = 0.12$).
The two instruments order the other two kinds differently.
The scorer certifies Beacon3D's sentence three times as often as ScanRefer's; GPT-6 resolves ScanRefer's a little more often (16 objects against 9, $p = 0.23$).
We have not tested why.
A likely reason is that ScanRefer's sentences are three times as long and say more about appearance, which the scorer does not credit and GPT-6 can see in the renderings.

Both arms of each model were asked with all 1,611 sentences, unchanged.
Before any result was in we fixed two things to report (\cref{tab:supp_b3d_models}).
The first is Beacon3D's measure by object, under which an object counts as right when the most specific sentence of each of its three chains is right, split by whether the object has a distractor.
We keep that way of grouping sentences but judge each answer by IoU, as on our own sets, where Beacon3D's script compares object identities; our figures are therefore not comparable with those of its paper.

The second is the 202 drawn sentences, with an exact McNemar test between the two arms of a model.

For the two models that choose among boxes the arms are level.

By object, with distractors, it is 28.7\% against 20.3\% at Acc@0.5 ($p = 0.012$) and 40.1\% against 40.6\% at Acc@0.25.

On original sentences the rewrite arm finds no more targets overall either (\cref{tab:supp_strata}); it leads where the sentence itself says more, on the rewrites and on the benchmark.
The lead needs the sentence as well as the training.

\section{The pipeline}
\label{supp:pipeline}
% OUTLINE (11-24; user: add pseudo-code, coarse grain, no thresholds).  Half a page.
The two procedures below give the pipeline at the grain of its steps, without thresholds.

\paragraph{Procedure 1: writing,} for a scene, a target set $T$ and, when there is one, an original sentence.
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Build the object table from the labelled point cloud: for each object its id, class, centre, bounding box and a sample of surface points.
\item Take as candidates $C$ every object whose class shares the head noun of the target's class; the distractors are $D = C \setminus T$.
\item Compute the evidence with the operators of \cref{supp:operators}: the nearest landmarks with their surface gaps, places to stand with the order of the candidates seen from each, what rests on what, the wall, corner or end of the room each candidate belongs to, and the list of distractors to exclude.
\item If the target has five or more distractors, add the rows, the furniture faces and the neighbours of every distractor.
\item Render the point cloud from several cameras with the objects marked by id, and draw a top-down outline of the room.
\item Call the writer once with the renderings, the object table, the evidence and the original sentence. It returns a sentence and the relations that sentence uses, written over object ids.
\item Keep the sentence as it is. The scorer's verdict is not returned to the writer.
\end{enumerate}
When a corpus has several sentences for the same targets, they are rewritten in one call.

\paragraph{Procedure 2: scoring,} for a sentence, a scene and the target set $T$, which is withheld until the last step.
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Call the reader once with the sentence, the list of every object in the room (id, class, position and size) and the predicate vocabulary of \cref{supp:vocab}.
\item The reader returns predicates over object ids that hold of everything the sentence describes, the parts that the sentence describes separately, each with its own predicates and stated count, and every clause it could not bind, with a reason.
\item For each predicate the verifier computes, from the object table and the points, the candidates that satisfy it.
\item Intersect the sets of the common predicates and of each part, and unite the parts (\cref{eq:parts}); the result is the referent $R$.
\item The sentence is certified when at least one predicate was evaluated and $R = T$. The strict score uses every predicate and every stated count; the lenient score first drops the predicates that hold for no target, and ignores counts.
\end{enumerate}
The writer knows the target and sees the scene.
The reader sees no image and is told neither the target, the candidates, the evidence nor the relations the writer returned.
The verifier sees predicates and geometry and no text.
Nothing passes from the scorer back to the writer.

\subsection{Prompts and configuration}
\label{supp:prompts}

% by section; the reader prompt may be given in full (it is short and defines the measurement).  One page.
% Reader prompt: Seshat-Star/reader/_extract_skills.py (OPEN, BLIND, RULES, OUT; inventory_blind; compose.PREDICATE_SPEC).
The writer, the reader and the translator used in the human check (\cref{supp:human}) are GPT-5.5; GPT-6 appears only as a test subject.
The writer's prompt is described in \cref{supp:writer}, where the passage that makes exclusion a requirement is quoted; when there is no original sentence the same prompt runs without the paragraphs that mention one (\cref{supp:data}).

The reader's prompt has three parts: its rules, the list of the room's objects, and the vocabulary of \cref{tab:supp_vocab}.
The rules that decide what is measured are these.
The reader records what the sentence claims and not what is true of the room, and does not repair a claim it believes wrong.
It binds a phrase to an object id only when the sentence names that object unambiguously; a phrase that could be several of the listed objects, or none, is left unbound.
A side word needs a viewpoint that the sentence itself gives.
Every relation the sentence states must appear in the answer, bound or unbound with a reason, and the reader reports how many relations the sentence states, which must equal the number it lists; a relation dropped in silence is the one failure that cannot be seen afterwards.

GPT-6 is given the sentence and four renderings of the scene with no labels or marks, and answers with a point (\cref{supp:external}); in the setting with labels it is given every object's label and position.

\subsection{Earlier attempts that failed}
\label{supp:failed}
% CLAIMS §3.3: relation menu + set cover + a small model to phrase the result (templated, relation-stuffed, little worth keeping);
% the feedback loop (scorer verdict returned to the writer), abandoned for the reason given in the pipeline section; "rich" evidence
% in uncrowded rooms (over-description) -> gating; two-pass writing.
% OUTLINE (11-24).  Half a column, text only.
\paragraph{A menu, a set cover and a small model.}
The first design let geometry decide what is said.
It listed the relations that hold for the target, chose by set cover a few that exclude every distractor, and had a small language model put them into a sentence.
The sentences were exact by construction and read like templates, one relation stacked on another, with nothing of what the annotator had said.
The present design reverses the roles: geometry supplies evidence, the writer chooses, and exactness is judged afterwards.

\paragraph{Returning the verdict to the writer.}
The pipeline can pass the verifier's verdict on a draft back to the writer for a second attempt.
We leave it switched off; no validation rewrite was written with it.
A sentence revised until the verifier accepts it tells us nothing further about the sentence, since the writer obeys what it is told (\cref{supp:writer}), and the verifier's score would cease to be independent of the text it scores.

\paragraph{All the evidence for every target.}
Given rows, furniture faces and the neighbours of every distractor for a target with one or two distractors, the writer described more than was needed and the sentences grew clumsy; read side by side on the development set, the leaner evidence was preferred.
These blocks are now given only when the target has five or more distractors.

\section{Operators}
\label{supp:operators}
% One paragraph per operator: what it computes, why it exists (the predicate names the writer used to invent before the operator
% existed: corner_of, head_of_table, ends_of_row ...), one real fact block.  Level of detail: what is computed, no thresholds.
% Source: CLAIMS §8 "算子选讲"; RESULTS §3.2 (rows: five revisions, each with its numbers; figure old vs new decomposition on
% scene0095_00 / scene0249_00 / scene0030_00), §4.5 (stack criterion, above / below).
No operator was right the first time.
Each revision began with a failure found by reading: the authors read some 1,500 rewrites in about 30 batches, over at least 245 scenes, next to the point cloud.
The row operator shows what such reading changes.
We first required a row to be straight.
Shown twelve computed rows, a human reader accepted eight and rejected four, and the rejected ones were among the straightest.
A person sees a row when its members stand apart from the other objects of their class; the rows that only the algorithm found did not. The operator now tests for that.
Rows, furniture faces and per-distractor neighbours are given to the writer only when the target has five or more distractors: given all of it for a target with few distractors, the writer described more than was needed to tell the target apart.
A development set of 49 scene--target units from the Multi3DRefer training split served these choices and enters no number we report.

\paragraph{What the verifier shares with the operators.}
The verifier and the operators that produce the writer's evidence share four measuring primitives---surface gap, support, collinearity and row decomposition---and a cache of surface samples; above these, neither calls the other.
An error in a primitive therefore shows on both sides, while an error above them stays on one, which is why the human check audits the verifier's output on its own.
Three of the four are textbook geometry; only the row decomposition involves judgement, which is why rows are the structure we test hardest.

% OUTLINE (11-24).  One and a half pages.
\paragraph{What each operator computes.}
% CLAIMS \S8 (operators, level of detail: what is computed, no thresholds); the paper's Evidence paragraph; spatial/compose.py.
\emph{Neighbours.}
For the target, and in a crowded room for each distractor, the operator lists the nearest objects of other classes with the gap between the two surfaces in metres, and for each such landmark the candidate nearest to it.
It gives measurements and no judgement: whether a gap counts as \emph{next to} is left to the writer, who sees the room.

\emph{Viewpoints.}
A side word needs a place to stand.
The operator proposes places of the form ``at $A$, facing $B$'', where $A$ and $B$ are objects of the room, and for each gives the order of the candidates from left to right as seen from there, with a note on how readable that order is from there.
A place to stand that is an object in the list is also one the reader can bind.

\emph{Furniture faces.}
For a large piece of furniture such as a table, a bed or a couch, the operator says at which face a candidate sits, a long side or a short end, and whether at a corner or along the middle.
A face is named by the landmark nearest to it, so that no standpoint is needed.

\emph{Stacks.}
The operator finds what rests on what from the overlap of two footprints and the heights of the two objects.
A stack counts as one seat in a row, and a horizontal relation is answered for the stack as a whole, since nothing horizontal tells its members apart; only the vertical relations do.

\emph{Room.}
From the outline of the room the operator gives the wall, the corner or the end of the room that a candidate belongs to.

\emph{Rows} are described next.
Before these operators existed the writer named such relations itself, with names such as \texttt{corner\_of}, \texttt{head\_of\_table} and \texttt{ends\_of\_row}, which the verifier could not evaluate.

\paragraph{Procedure 3: rows,} for the candidates of one class.
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\item Fold every stack into one seat.
\item From each pair of neighbouring seats grow a chain: add at either end the next seat only if it is among the nearest ones and at a spacing like that of the chain so far; the direction is refitted to the chain at every step.
\item Cut off an end seat that lies off the line of the chain and is an outlier among its members.
\item Score every chain, chiefly by its isolation, that is, by how few other objects of the class stand loose around its members. A long chain must also hold together; straightness and the other quantities enter the score and are not conditions of their own.
\item Order the seats along each row and name each end of a row by the landmark nearest to it; number the rows across the room.
\item Report the candidates in no row as a loose group.
\end{enumerate}
The block that results has this form:
{\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
ROW LAYOUT: of the 9 chairs, 7 stand in 2
 straight rows, listed in order across the room
 - row 1 is the one nearest the whiteboard #3,
   row 2 the one nearest the door #8
 the target sits in the 2nd row of 2, counting
 from the end nearest the whiteboard #3
 row 1 of 2: #4=exclude, #5=exclude, #6=exclude
 row 2 of 2: #7=exclude, #12=IN-SET, #9=exclude,
   #10=exclude - the target is 2nd of 4
 a loose group of 2, not in any row,
   beside the table #1: #13=exclude, #14=exclude
\end{verbatim}
}
\noindent The block also says what it cannot know: it is computed from the centres of footprints and can say which objects line up and in what order, not whether the line reads as a row in the picture, and it asks the writer to find the row in the rendering before counting.

\subsection{Sentences with several targets}
\label{supp:parts}
% Source: spatial/compose.py verify_reader_parts (blind reader) / verify_parts (writer).
A sentence about several objects often coordinates groups, each pinned by its own relations (\emph{the two at the head of the near bed and the one on the far bed}).
The reader therefore returns \emph{common} predicates, which hold for the whole referent, and a list of \emph{parts}, each with its own predicates and, when the sentence states one, a count.
Let $B$ be the candidates that satisfy every common predicate ($B = C$ if there is none), and $S_j$ the members of $B$ that satisfy every predicate of part $j$.
The referent is the union of the parts,
\begin{equation}
  R = \bigcup_j S_j ,
  \label{eq:parts}
\end{equation}
or $R = B$ when the sentence has no parts, and the sentence is certified when at least one predicate was evaluated and $R = T$.
Union, not intersection: the pillow on the left bed need not be on the right bed, so an object joins the referent by satisfying one part.
Parts may overlap, and a part may pick out fewer members than its wording suggests, provided another part covers the rest.
No part may admit a distractor: an object that satisfies one part is in $R$.
The strict score also requires every stated count to equal the size of the set it describes; the lenient score skips predicates that hold for no target and ignores counts.
A single-target sentence is the special case of no parts and $|T| = 1$, which gives \cref{eq:exact}.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition 1 and sensitivity}
\label{supp:proof}
% Source: Seshat-Star/docs/VALIDATION_AND_EXPERIMENTS.md §1.2; RESULTS §4.1--4.2 (numbers after the 11-12 correction).
Proposition 1 of the paper says that a sentence the verifier certifies is exact, provided the verifier makes neither of two kinds of error.
Recall that $\hat{E}_k$ is the set of candidates the verifier returns for predicate $p_k$ and $E_k$ the set that truly satisfies it; $T$ is the target set and $D$ the distractors.
The sentence is certified when $\bigcap_k \hat{E}_k = T$ and exact when $\bigcap_k E_k = T$.
Assume (A1) $\hat{E}_k \cap T \subseteq E_k$, no false positive on a target, and (A2) $E_k \cap D \subseteq \hat{E}_k$, no false negative on a distractor, for every $k$, and $\bigcap_k \hat{E}_k = T$.

\emph{$T \subseteq \bigcap_k E_k$.}
Take $t \in T$.
Since $T = \bigcap_k \hat{E}_k$, $t \in \hat{E}_k$ for every $k$, hence $t \in \hat{E}_k \cap T \subseteq E_k$ by (A1).

\emph{$\bigcap_k E_k \subseteq T$.}
Take $x \in \bigcap_k E_k$ and suppose $x \notin T$, so $x \in D$.
Then $x \in E_k \cap D \subseteq \hat{E}_k$ for every $k$ by (A2), hence $x \in \bigcap_k \hat{E}_k = T$, a contradiction. \hfill$\square$

The converse fails: if a distractor truly satisfies every predicate and the verifier wrongly rejects it on one, the sentence is certified and is not exact.
That is a violation of (A2), and it is the error to guard against.
(A1) and (A2) restrict errors in opposite directions on complementary sets, so a bound on the verifier's overall error rate implies neither.
The proposition takes the predicates as given and does not cover the reader.

\paragraph{How fragile is a certificate?}
For a certified sentence and a distractor $d$, let $m(d)$ be the number of predicates that exclude $d$.
If the verifier wrongly excludes a distractor with probability at most $q$ per predicate, independently across predicates, the probability that the certificate is false is at most
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{d \in D} q^{\,m(d)} ,
  \label{eq:margin}
\end{equation}
by a union bound over distractors.
On the Multi3DRefer validation rewrites, 13.5\% of the distractors of certified sentences are excluded by a single predicate and 58.2\% by three or more; 17.0\% of certified sentences have a distractor that hangs on one predicate.
\Cref{eq:margin}, averaged over these sentences, gives at most 3.5\%, 7.2\% and 14.5\% false certificates for $q = 0.05$, $0.10$ and $0.20$, most of it from the sentences with a single-predicate distractor.
% NOTE: this analysis runs on the WRITER's own relations (sealed val, 3,626 certified), not on the blind reader's; say so if the
% paragraph is kept, and connect q to the human predicate check (near misses) once that table is final.
These figures are computed on the relations stored with each rewrite, the writer's own list, and not on the blind reader's.
For $q$ the human check gives a loose guide, and only for rows, furniture faces and stacks: of the statements about them that the verifier rejects, annotators judged true 4 of 30 clear cases and 13 of 41 near misses (\cref{tab:supp_human_b}).
Near misses were sampled on purpose and an annotator can be wrong, so these shares are above what $q$ would be on the statements a rewrite makes; for the other predicates $q$ is not measured.

\subsection{Corrections to the verifier}
\label{supp:ruler}
% RESULTS §4.5: above / below with a same-class reference (405 stacked pairs returned the empty set); the stack criterion;
% 11-12 surface gap (boxes vs points: scene0671 chair, 0.41 m by boxes, 1.04 m on the points; 9.5% of 444 closest / farthest claims
% in 141 counterfactual pairs change winner; certified rate of sealed rewrites 72.9 -> 69.4 and 74.3 -> 71.3 on the writer's
% relations; judge600 rewrites 57.3 -> 54.2 and 58.0 -> 54.8, originals within one point).  Training data were not regenerated.
The verifier has been wrong before, and each time a human reader caught it.
In the latest case it measured the gap between two objects between their bounding boxes and not between their surfaces, so that a corner of the box of an object standing at an angle reached towards a landmark the object itself was far from.
In one scene a chair was 0.41\,m from a cabinet by its box and 1.04\,m by its points, and was judged the closest to the cabinet although the three other chairs were 0.61 to 0.66\,m away.
Measured on 444 \emph{closest} and \emph{farthest} claims, the two ways of measuring disagree on the winner in 9.5\%.
The verifier now measures between surface points.
The certified rates in the paper all follow the correction; on the sample of the paper's data table it lowered the certified rate of the rewrites by about three points (57.3 to 54.2 on Multi3DRefer, 58.0 to 54.8 on ScanRefer) and moved that of the originals by less than one.

% OUTLINE (11-24).  Half a column.
Two earlier corrections were of the same kind.
\emph{Above} and \emph{below} once refused a reference object of the target's own class, so that \emph{the towel above the other towel} could not be evaluated: every one of the 405 stacked pairs in the validation set returned the empty set, and the sentence failed.
After the correction 383 validation sentences gained a certificate and 21 lost one.
A stack was once recognised by fixed horizontal and vertical distances; it is now recognised by the overlap of the two footprints and by the upper object standing no lower than the top of the lower one.
The case that exposed it was a scene in which six chairs stand in three stacks of two, described by its annotator as six stacked chairs, three on the top and three on the bottom; the present test finds the three stacks.
Errors of this kind show on cases near a boundary, which is why half of the false statements in the human check are near misses (\cref{supp:human}).
The training data were written before the last correction and were not regenerated: on the writer's own relations, 4.5\% of the Multi3DRefer validation rewrites and 4.0\% of ScanRefer's lost their certificate under it, which we take as an estimate of the share of training sentences that state a ranking relation which does not hold.
